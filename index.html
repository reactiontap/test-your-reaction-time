Three Types of Reaction Time Test

Scientists split reaction time into different types because the brain does different work in each. A reaction time test that covers all three gives you a much fuller picture than one number.

01

Simple reaction time

One signal, one response. You already know what to do, you just wait for “now”. This is the classic reflex test and gives the fastest scores, usually about 200–280 ms online.

02

Choice reaction time

Several possible signals, each with its own response. Your brain must work out which colour lit up before your hand moves, so expect to be 80–150 ms slower than in the simple test.

03

Go/no-go reaction time

Respond to one signal and hold still for another. It measures speed and self-control together. Rushing makes you faster but brings more mistakes, so accuracy matters as much as time.

Why the Choice Reaction Time Test Is Slower: Hick's Law

In the 1950s, psychologists William Hick and Ray Hyman showed that reaction time grows as the number of possible choices grows. It doesn't double with every extra option. It rises by roughly the same amount every time the number of choices doubles. Going from one choice to two adds a noticeable delay; going from two to four adds about the same again.

This is known as Hick's law, and designers use it every day. Fewer menu buttons, clear colours and familiar layouts help people react faster. It's also why games feel harder when more things happen at once, even if your basic reflexes haven't changed.

How to Take a Reaction Time Test Properly

  1. Start with the simple reaction time test. It gives your baseline speed.
  2. Then try the choice test. The difference from your simple score is your “decision time”.
  3. Finish with go/no-go. Look at accuracy first, then speed.
  4. Repeat on another day. One session is a snapshot. Three sessions on different days show your true range.

Use the same device and the same input each time. A wired mouse or keyboard is quicker than a touch screen, and a 120 Hz or 144 Hz display shows the signal sooner than a standard 60 Hz one.

A 7-Day Plan to Improve Your Reaction Time Test Score

Short, regular practice works better than one long session. Spend about five minutes a day, then take the reaction time test again on day 7 and compare it with day 1.

Day 1

Do all three tests twice. Write down your averages as a baseline.

Day 2

Simple test ×3. Keep your finger resting lightly and eyes on the centre.

Day 3

Choice test ×3. Learn where each colour sits so you don't have to look for it.

Day 4

Rest from the tests. Play a fast sport, catch a ball or play a quick game.

Day 5

Go/no-go ×3. Aim for 100% accuracy before you try to get faster.

Day 6

Mix all three. Try a click-speed or memory game for variety.

Day 7

Test at the same time as day 1, after a good night's sleep, and compare.

Two more ways to train

Speed and focus go together. Try a CPS test to see how many clicks per second you can manage, then a Stroop effect test, where you name the ink colour instead of reading the word. Both push the same quick-decision skills as the choice and go/no-go modes of this reaction time test.

What Slows Your Reaction Time Test Result

  • Lack of sleep: the biggest everyday factor. Even one short night makes scores slower and less even.
  • Distraction: phones, music and conversation split your attention.
  • Alcohol and some medicines: these can slow reactions a lot, which is why they matter for driving.
  • Cold hands: stiff fingers move more slowly. Warm them up first.
  • Age: simple reaction time is usually fastest in your twenties and slows gradually after that, though practice helps.

Who Uses a Reaction Time Test?

Goalkeepers, tennis players, boxers and racing drivers all depend on fast and accurate responses. So do esports players, where a few milliseconds can decide a fight. In daily life, quick reactions help when driving, cycling or catching something you dropped. Coaches, driving instructors and researchers use a reaction time test to check alertness and track progress. For most of these, choice and go/no-go skills matter more than a single fast tap, which is exactly why this test has all three modes.

Reaction Time Test FAQs

How does this reaction time test work?

Pick a mode, tap to start and react as soon as the signal appears. The simple test measures pure speed, the choice test adds picking the right colour, and the go/no-go test checks speed and self-control. Each gives an average in milliseconds.

What is the difference between simple and choice reaction time?

Simple reaction time has one signal and one response. Choice reaction time has several signals, each with its own response, so the brain needs extra time to decide. Choice times are usually 80–150 ms slower.

What does the go/no-go reaction time test measure?

It measures how quickly you respond to a 'go' signal and how well you hold back on a 'no-go' signal. Psychologists use it to study attention and impulse control. Accuracy is as important as speed.

What is Hick's law?

Hick's law says reaction time rises as the number of choices rises, by roughly the same amount each time the number of choices doubles. It explains why the 4-colour test is slower than the simple test.

What is a good reaction time test score?

On a simple online reaction time test, most adults average 200–280 ms. On a 4-choice test, 320–450 ms is common. Compare your own results over several days rather than with other people.

Can I use the keyboard?

Yes. Press the space bar for the simple and go/no-go tests. In the choice test, use A, S, D and F (or 1–4) for the four pads, from top-left to bottom-right.

Why did I get 'Wrong pad' or 'Oops, hold'?

In the choice test you tapped a pad that wasn't lit, and in the go/no-go test you tapped on a red cross. These trials count against your accuracy and don't add a time.

Where are my scores saved?

Your best average for each test is saved only in your own browser so you can compare next time. Nothing is uploaded, and the reset button clears it.
